# Land reclamation, wetland loss and flooding in Lagos: a review of the evidence

Since 1984 Lagos has lost a large share of the mangrove, swamp and open water around its lagoons. Most of it has been filled with dredged sand and built on. The best-documented figure comes from the eastern lagoon fringe: mangroves there fell from about 88.5 km² to 20 km² (−77%) and swamps from 345 km² to 165 km² (−52%) between 1984 and 2006. Statewide, built-up land rose from about 15% to 53% of Lagos State between 1984 and 2023. Researchers, residents and the state government all link these losses to worse flooding. They point to three mechanisms: floodplain storage is removed, drainage outfalls that already struggle at high tide are blocked, and new development sits on soft fill only slightly above lagoon level. A plot-scale feedback loop, in which flooded owners raise their land and push water onto neighbours, is well described in testimony but not yet measured. To 2050, the published evidence points to continued urban expansion, local land subsidence large enough to rival climate-driven sea-level rise, and heavier rainfall extremes whose clearest signal comes after mid-century. The literature has clear weaknesses. It consists of two- and three-date snapshots with inconsistent class definitions, several studies report no accuracy assessment, the studies disagree on even the direction of wetland change, and no study has converted lost wetland area into lost flood-storage volume. This study aims to fill these gaps with free Landsat and Sentinel data, Random Forest classification in Google Earth Engine, stratified area estimation, a DEM-based storage calculation and a validated land-change model to 2050.

## How to read the evidence in this report

This review was carried out in November 2026. Each source is marked as **verified** (the full text, publisher page or official catalog entry was read and the figure checked) or **unverified** (known only from search-engine summaries, secondary reporting, a page that could not be opened, or cited from secondary literature without the original being checked). In the text below, a figure from an unverified source is labelled as such. The reference list at the end is split into verified and unverified sources. Several important primary documents returned access errors and could not be checked: the World Bank coastal-degradation report, the 2024 paper delineating reclamation in Eti-Osa and Lagos Island, the 2025 Lagos subsidence and sea-level paper, and the Ohenhen and Shirzaei subsidence paper. These will be retrieved and checked before the analysis is finalised. Where studies conflict, the conflict is stated rather than averaged away.

## Lagos lost most of its lagoon-fringe wetland, but studies disagree on how much

### The eastern lagoon fringe: the best-documented losses

The anchor study is Obiefuna, Nwilo, Atagbaza and Okolie (2013). They compared Landsat TM imagery from December 1984 with ETM+ imagery from December 2006 over the eastern half of Lagos State, about 2,561 km² around the Lagos and Lekki lagoons. **Mangroves fell from 88.51 km² to 19.95 km², a loss of 68.6 km² (−77.5%, about 3.1 km² a year). Swamps fell from 344.75 km² to 165.37 km², a loss of 179.4 km² (−52%, about 8.2 km² a year).** Combined, the wetland classes shrank from about 433 km² to 185 km² ([Obiefuna et al. 2013](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8dcd/cc887242c5612ee641e61836952bb132f20e.pdf)). Losses were concentrated in Epe, Eti-Osa, Ibeju-Lekki and Ikorodu. Mangrove loss was fastest in Epe and Eti-Osa, and swamp loss fastest in Epe and Ibeju-Lekki. The authors trace early mangrove loss in western Eti-Osa to the Lekki Peninsula Residential Scheme of the 1980s. They also document reclamation around Omu Creek and residential pressure on the swamps north of the Lekki–Epe Expressway. By 2006 the remaining mangrove was mostly on the northern shore of Lagos Lagoon (Ikorodu, Kosofe) and in Epe, which means the southern Lekki fringe had largely been converted.

This study has important limits. It used a simple parallelepiped classifier and **reports no accuracy assessment**. It only covers the eastern half of the state, so Badagry, Ologe Lagoon and Ojo are excluded. Its tables contain small internal inconsistencies, for example a 2006 mangrove area of 19.95 km² in one place and 19.92 km² in another ([Obiefuna et al. 2013](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8dcd/cc887242c5612ee641e61836952bb132f20e.pdf)). The same team published a companion paper in the *Journal of Coastal Research* in the same year ([BioOne](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Coastal-Research/volume-29/issue-3/JCOASTRES-D-12-00038.1/Land-Cover-Dynamics-Associated-with-the-Spatial-Changes-in-the/10.2112/JCOASTRES-D-12-00038.1.full)). Its existence and DOI are verified, but its figures were not checked. One abstract describes its classification as unsupervised, which conflicts with the parallelepiped method in the JSD paper.

Older work in the lower Ogun floodplain, which drains into Lagos Lagoon, points the same way over a longer period. Odunuga and Oyebande (2007) compared 1965 aerial photographs with 2005 Landsat imagery. They found wetland falling from **82% to 36% of the floodplain**, while built-up land rose from 1% to 30% ([Odunuga & Oyebande 2007](https://iahs.info/uploads/dms/14109.17-91-99-10-Odunuga.pdf)). The paper prints its total area as 38.01 × 10⁶ ha, which would be 380,000 km² and is clearly a unit error (probably ×10³ ha). Its percentages are usable, but its absolute areas are not, and it too reports no classification accuracy.

### Statewide studies give a different and less consistent picture

The most recent statewide study is Aniramu, Iyanda and Orimoogunje (2026) in *Scientific Reports*. They classified Landsat imagery for 1984, 2002 and 2023 using Maximum Likelihood, with reported overall accuracies of 92–97% and kappa of 0.90–0.96. **Built-up land rose from 15.49% to 53.17% of the state, about 3,370 ha a year. Water fell from 25.04% to 13.78%. Forest fell from 46.55% to 13.74%. Wetland fell from 12.92% to 8.93%** ([Aniramu et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12960691/)). Mongabay reported in September 2026 that a February 2026 *Scientific Reports* study found built-up area grew by 38%, forest fell by 32% and water bodies by 11% between 1984 and 2023. It also reported the authors' projection that more than 60% of Lagos urban areas would face extreme flooding by 2050 ([Mongabay 2026](https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2026/09/rapid-wetland-loss-and-real-estate-boom-increasing-flood-risk-in-lagos/)). Those three numbers match the percentage-point changes in Aniramu et al. almost exactly (+37.7, −32.8 and −11.3 points). This makes it very likely that Mongabay was describing this paper and that its "percent" means percentage points of the state's area. This identification is an inference, and the 60% projection was not checked against the paper.

The Aniramu paper has problems that matter for anyone citing it. Its wetland class *rose* by about 34,000 ha between 1984 and 2002, to 22.4% of the state, before falling by about 48,000 ha to 2023. Its text gives a wetland decline of 4.32% where its table implies 4.0 points. It states the study area as both 357,700 ha and 3,770 km². Its 2050 projection puts wetland at 39,980 ha, *more* than in 2023, which contradicts the paper's own argument that flood-buffering capacity is declining ([Aniramu et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12960691/)). Gilbert and Shi (2023) studied the Ikeja–Ikorodu–Epe–Badagry ("IBILE") region from 2000 to 2020. They found built-up land more than doubling, from 598 km² to 1,271 km², but a small *net gain* in wetland of 2.77 km² (a loss of 28 km² in 2000–2010, then a gain of 31 km² in 2010–2020). Their percentages do not reconcile with their areas ([Gilbert & Shi 2023](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=130283)).

The apparent wetland gains in the statewide studies are most plausibly artefacts rather than real wetland creation. Possible causes are forest being reclassified as wetland, imagery from different seasons with different water levels, or plain misclassification. In Aniramu et al., wetland is the class with the lowest accuracy, with producer's and user's accuracies of about 84–86% in 1984 according to search summaries. None of the papers tests these explanations. The disagreement is itself a research finding: three peer-reviewed studies covering overlapping territory and periods do not agree on whether Lagos wetland area went up or down between the mid-1980s and early 2000s.

### Eti-Osa magnitudes do not reconcile

An unverified 2018 *Applied Geomatics* paper on Eti-Osa reports that **6,717.65 ha (67 km²) of mangrove** was lost there between 1984 and 2013, along with 1,314 ha of wetland and 1,740 ha of water. It values the lost ecosystem services at about US$101.6 million a year ([Springer, unverified](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12518-018-0242-2)). Obiefuna et al. put the total 1984 mangrove area in Eti-Osa at only 22.65 km² ([Obiefuna et al. 2013](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8dcd/cc887242c5612ee641e61836952bb132f20e.pdf)). A loss three times larger than the verified starting stock cannot both be true under the same definition. The likeliest explanation is that the 2018 paper counted all swamp forest as "mangrove" or used a different boundary. Other unverified figures that circulate include a fall from 13,730 ha to 2,270 ha of mangrove ("vegetation") in a 1984–2014 Lagos study ([ResearchGate, attribution unconfirmed](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349519800_Analyzing_urban_growth_and_land_cover_change_scenario_in_Lagos_Nigeria_using_multi-temporal_remote_sensing_data_and_GIS_to_mitigate_flooding)). Another is about 285 ha of mangrove lost on the Lekki coast between 1998 and 2018 ([ResearchGate, authors unconfirmed](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377352092_Effect_of_Localized_Development_on_Land_Use_and_Coastline_Dynamics_Focus_on_Recent_Changes_Along_the_Lekki_Peninsula)). A third is a loss of more than 80% of the wetlands typical of Lekki Peninsula and Festac Town, from an early aerial-photo study ([AMS 2000](https://ams.confex.com/ams/AugDavis/techprogram/paper_15620.htm)). None of these should be quoted as established until the source papers are read.

### Megaproject reclamation is documented mostly by developers and the press

Eko Atlantic is the largest single reclamation. It is built on land taken from the Atlantic in front of Bar Beach, not from wetland, so it should be kept separate from wetland-loss totals. Its planned area is about 9–10 km² (around 1,000 ha). Reported progress is about 500 ha by 2015 and 65% complete in February 2021. A Wikipedia account puts it at about two-thirds (more than 6.5 million m²) by June 2023, using more than 75 million m³ of sand. All of these figures come from the developer, the engineer or the press, and all are unverified ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eko_Atlantic); [ThisDay 2021](https://thisdaylive.com/index.php/2021/02/02/eko-atlantic-city-attains-65-reclamation); [Royal HaskoningDHV](https://royalhaskoningdhv.com/en/projects/a-new-coastal-city-built-on-reclaimed-land-from-the-sea)). No independent satellite measurement of the reclaimed area was found. No reliable published area was found for Banana Island, Lekki Phase 1 or the Lekki Free Trade Zone. The state's own record shows that encroachment continues: it suspended further approvals at Banana Island and Osborne Foreshore in 2021, and demolished or marked illegal fill there in 2023–2025 ([Nairametrics 2021](https://nairametrics.com/2021/02/11/lagos-re-channels-re-aligns-banana-island-shoreline-says-demolition-continues/); [NAN 2023](https://nannews.ng/2023/10/21/lagos-govt-demolishes-illegal-banana-island-structure-warns-against-unapproved-dredging)). A September 2025 headline reports that the Lagos landmass has grown from 3,577 to 4,050 km² through reclamation. That would be an extraordinary 13% increase, and it is unverified ([Nairametrics 2025](https://nairametrics.com/2025/09/22/lagos-landmass-expands-from-3577-to-4050-sq-km-driven-by-massive-reclamation/)). The most relevant academic source is a 2024 paper delineating reclamation areas in Eti-Osa and Lagos Island from 1992 to 2022 ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/6bbb88374a734a77aa48705c6681b7e9); [AgEcon Search](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/342069)). Its figures could not be retrieved, and obtaining them is a first step for this study.

### Dredging is the unmeasured half of reclamation

The sand used for filling comes largely from the Lagos and Lekki lagoons and inland creeks, so dredging and reclamation are best treated as one system. An Associated Press investigation in January 2026 described dredging as a booming business driven by demand for sand for concrete and reclamation. Researchers it interviewed linked dredging to high turbidity that disrupts fish breeding and to the loss of natural lagoon buffers, but the report gave no figures for sites, permits or volumes ([Africanews/AP 2026](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/01/12/lagos-dredging-boom-fuels-development-but-sinks-fishing-livelihoods)). A field comparison of dredged and undredged sites at Ibeshe, Ikorodu, found higher turbidity and nitrate at dredged sites (Adekunbi et al. 2018, unverified) ([typeset.io](https://typeset.io/pdf/impact-of-sand-dredging-activities-on-ecosystem-and-9nyia8rysp.pdf)). A widely shared claim that the lagoon bed dropped by nearly 6 m along a 5 km stretch has no primary source and should not be used ([Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/mining/news/the-water-is-no-longer-our-friend-how-dredging-is-pushing-lagos-lagoon-towards-ecosystem-collapse-ph-20c0ae31-caea-42ad-9a23-cefc551c544e/)). No credible figures were found for dredged volumes or the number of operators, and no bathymetric time series exists for Lagos Lagoon. Obiefuna et al. noted in 2013 that published bathymetric data seemed to be lacking ([Obiefuna et al. 2013](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8dcd/cc887242c5612ee641e61836952bb132f20e.pdf)).

### The open coast: harbour moles and Eko Atlantic shift erosion east

On the Atlantic shore, the harbour moles at Commodore Channel, built from 1907 onwards, interrupt the eastward longshore drift. Sand builds up at Lighthouse Beach to the west, and Bar Beach and Victoria Island to the east erode. Reported Bar Beach retreat rates are about 1.7 m/yr recently and 2.1–2.3 m/yr earlier ([Hydro International, unverified](https://www.hydro-international.com/content/article/coastline-migration-in-nigeria)). Estimates of total retreat conflict: 178 m from 1900 to 2002 in one source against more than 1 km over about a century in a TU Delft thesis ([TU Delft, unverified](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_446fa518-0982-4003-95af-8e6ba4dfa5ff)). The two figures probably refer to different reference lines or segments, but this has not been resolved. A 2012 modelling study using UNIBEST (Bentum et al., unverified) concluded that Eko Atlantic would not change total erosion volumes downdrift, but that its revetment would move the erosion further east ([U. Twente proceedings](https://proceedings.utwente.nl/199/)). Residents of Okun Alfa and Alpha Beach report severe land loss, which the developer denies causing ([Phys.org/AFP 2019](https://phys.org/news/2019-07-nigeria-lagos-atlantic-erosion.pdf)). Lagos-wide shoreline studies, all unverified, give mean retreat of about 1.8–2.8 m/yr, with local erosion of up to about 25 m/yr on the Okun-Ajah/Ibeju-Lekki axis ([Mersin IGD](https://publish.mersin.edu.tr/index.php/igd/article/view/394); [Geomatics, Natural Hazards and Risk 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/10.1080/19475705.2026.2688688)). One circulating figure of −56 m/yr appears to refer to communities on the Ondo/Delta coast, not Lagos, and should be excluded ([ITS IJMEIR](https://iptek.its.ac.id/index.php/ijmeir/article/download/12563/pdf_95)).

## Reclamation turns rainfall and tide into compound floods

### Three physical mechanisms, inferred more than measured

The literature argues for a three-part causal chain. Filling wetland and lagoon margins removes storage that would otherwise absorb storm runoff and high lagoon stages. Paving increases runoff. And fill placed across natural flow paths blocks drainage. The evidence for this chain is mostly co-occurrence: land-cover change happening alongside more flooding, plus physical reasoning. **No peer-reviewed hydrological or hydrodynamic model was found that isolates the effect of reclamation on flood depth or extent in Lagos, and no study has quantified the lost storage volume in cubic metres.** Aniramu et al. link wetland and forest conversion to emerging flood zones by spatial correlation only ([Aniramu et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12960691/)). A 2026 study of Lekki reports that between 2015 and 2025 vegetation fell by 13%, water bodies by 6%, and settlements grew by 10% onto natural cover, and the authors link this to more flooding (reported by [Mongabay 2026](https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2026/09/rapid-wetland-loss-and-real-estate-boom-increasing-flood-risk-in-lagos/); the underlying paper is unverified). A FIG Working Week 2026 abstract on reclaimed land in Eti-Osa/Lekki reports that intense rainfall combined with tidal fluctuation makes reclaimed land more prone to waterlogging and instability (unverified) ([FIG 2026](https://fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2026/papers/ts03c/TS03C_ojo_herbert_13988_abs.pdf)).

The tidal element matters because Lagos's primary drains discharge by gravity into the lagoon and the sea. When heavy rain coincides with high tide or a high lagoon stage, the outfalls back up. The July 2011 flood is the best-documented case. Reported rainfall was about 233 mm in 24 hours at Ikeja (158% of the July mean) in one account and about 260 mm in 18 hours in another. Officials said high tide had blocked the channels, and at least 20 people died (all unverified; [NHESS 2016](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/16/349/2016/nhess-16-349-2016.pdf); [Al Jazeera 2011](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2011/7/13/flash-flooding-hits-lagos)). The two rainfall figures cover different durations, so they need not conflict, but neither was checked against station records. No event data were retrieved for the 2012, 2017, 2020 or 2021 floods. No joint-probability analysis of rainfall and tide or lagoon level for Lagos was found. Reclaimed ground adds to the compound hazard in two ways. It removes the lagoon-margin storage that buffers high stages, and it places buildings on sand over soft lagoonal clay, which compacts over time and lowers the margin above flood level. The second point is supported by subsidence evidence discussed below.

### The feedback loop: well described, not yet quantified

The idea that flooding drives more filling, which drives more flooding, works at three scales in Lagos. At the **plot scale**, a flooded owner sand-fills or raises a plot or road, displacing ponded water onto lower neighbours and drains, who then raise their own land. Residents' associations in Lekki–Epe, Ikoyi and Victoria Island have described exactly this. They report that neighbouring sand-filling blocked drainage and pushed water into homes, and they have asked the state to halt sand-filling and enforce the drainage master plan ([Guardian Nigeria, unverified](https://guardian.ng/news/lekki-property-owners-propose-end-to-sand-filling-flood-management-strategies/); [Nigeria Housing Market, unverified](https://www.nigeriahousingmarket.com/news/residents-call-for-probe-into-ikoyi-lagoon-reclamation-project)). At the **coast scale**, hard structures such as the moles and the Eko Atlantic revetment fix the shoreline locally and move the sediment deficit east. That creates demand for new protection further along the coast, as the Eko Atlantic modelling implies. A **material-supply link** joins the two: the sand for filling is dredged from the lagoon, which degrades the lagoon's own capacity to absorb water.

All three loops are supported by physical mechanism and by testimony, but **no peer-reviewed Lagos study has measured or modelled any of them.** Analogues show the pattern elsewhere. In Jakarta, subsidence driven by groundwater extraction (about 3.5 cm/yr on average, and up to 4 m in total in the north) has led to sea walls that critics say do not address the cause. In Ho Chi Minh City, filling of ponds and canals and raising of roads have reduced water retention ([Vietnam News 2026](https://vietnamnews.vn/world/1763706/jakarta-sinks-deeper-as-subsidence-worsens-flood-risks.html); [VietNamNet](https://vietnamnet.vn/en/extreme-flooding-in-ho-chi-minh-city-experts-uncover-the-root-causes-2463547.html); both unverified). Turning the Lagos loop from testimony into measurement is one of the clearest contributions a new study could make.

### Policy has moved toward land-use control, but enforcement is weak

The policy record shows the state treating reclamation as a flood driver. In September 2025 Lagos reportedly suspended all reclamation projects, citing a surge of filling on wetlands, floodplains and lagoon fronts without environmental impact assessment or drainage clearance ([AllAfrica 2025, unverified](https://allafrica.com/stories/202509180458.html)). The same month it began clearing buildings from the Ikota River right of way in Lekki, and announced plans for pumped drainage cells and wetland restoration ([Nairametrics 2025, unverified](https://nairametrics.com/2025/09/30/lagos-begins-clearance-of-illegal-buildings-on-ikota-river-right-of-way)). Critics point to weak implementation. A Heinrich Böll Foundation position paper reports no enforcement of the drainage master plan in newly developed areas and recommends a law that would penalise filling of buffer zones ([Böll 2019, unverified](https://ng.boell.org/sites/default/files/flood-wetland-management_2019.pdf)). Commentators say that fewer than a quarter of master-plan components are implemented before the plans expire ([Tribune, unverified](https://tribuneonlineng.com/built-on-water-why-lagos-keeps-flooding/)). Jurisdiction is also contested: the state environment ministry said it had not approved filling in Ikoyi Lagoon and that the matter was outside its purview ([ThisDay 2023, unverified](https://thisdaylive.com/index.php/2023/12/18/land-speculators-illegally-sand-filling-ikoyi-lagoon-for-housing-devt-fuelling-climate-crisis)). The Lagos Resilience Strategy (2020) itself was not retrieved. It is not known whether the 2025 suspension is still in force.

The economic stakes are large but poorly sourced. A World Bank study (Croitoru et al. 2020) is widely reported to put the combined cost of flooding, erosion and pollution in Lagos, Delta and Cross River states at about US$9.7 billion in 2018, or 2.4% of Nigeria's GDP, with flooding and erosion making up more than 60% of that. The report could not be opened, so these figures are unverified ([World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/34758)).

## By 2050, subsidence may matter more than the emissions scenario

### Sea-level rise: Lagos-specific AR6 values still need extracting

The exact IPCC AR6 projections for the Lagos grid cell were not retrieved. They will be taken directly from the NASA/IPCC AR6 Sea Level Projection Tool: select the 1° grid cell offshore of Lagos (around 6.4°N, 3.4°E), set medium confidence, read the median and the 17th–83rd percentile range for 2050 and 2100 under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5, and download the CSV, which includes the vertical land motion term ([NASA sea level](https://sealevel.nasa.gov/news/224/nasa-international-panel-provide-a-new-window-on-rising-seas/)). For context, the AR6 *global-mean* medians for 2100 relative to 1995–2014 are about 0.44 m (SSP1-2.6), 0.56 m (SSP2-4.5) and 0.77 m (SSP5-8.5). These values are quoted from the AR6 Summary for Policymakers and still need to be re-checked against the original. The spread between scenarios is small by 2050 and widens sharply after it. A Gulf of Guinea study based on CMIP6 projects 1,458–4,332 km² of permanently inundated land region-wide by 2100, with no Lagos breakdown (Ghomsi et al. 2024, unverified) ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/90a25c54b1c04e459eea97e4211cec8d)). Popular claims that Lagos will be underwater by 2050, including an end-of-century range of 1–2.5 m attributed to a pessimistic scenario ([Earth.org](https://earth.org/data_visualization/sea-level-rise-by-2100-lagos/)), are not AR6 values and should not be used.

### Subsidence: credible rates are millimetres a year, fastest on new fill

Local land movement probably changes the picture more than scenario choice does before 2050. A precursor study presented at the 2023 AGU meeting combined satellite measurements of sea-level rise, tides and land motion to produce Lagos flood projections for 2030–2100 with and without subsidence. It found the greatest subsidence in less-populated areas around the city ([CIRES 2023](https://cires.colorado.edu/news/scientists-discover-land-subsidence-may-contribute-more-coastal-flooding-previously-thought)). A related 2025 study, which is unverified and whose authors and journal were not identified, used GNSS-referenced Sentinel-1 InSAR for 2015–2025. It reports vertical rates of −11.4 to +0.8 mm/yr, finds that land motion brings flooding forward by 48–60 years, and estimates that under SSP2-4.5 the 2100 flood extent in south-west Lagos rises by 70% (from 0.98 to 1.68 km²) once subsidence is included ([PreventionWeb](https://www.preventionweb.net/quick/104143)). Ohenhen and Shirzaei (2022) report rates above 4 mm/yr, with hotspots above 6 mm/yr in Lekki, south-west Lagos and floodplains (2018–2021). They put 5–81 km² and 255–4,000 buildings at high to very high risk of future collapse (unverified figures; [DOI 10.1029/2022EF003219](https://zendy.io/title/10.1029/2022ef003219)).

Older studies report much larger line-of-sight rates, up to −87 to −94 mm/yr, and attribute the highest rates to heavy structures on landfill ([Springer 2021, unverified](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12665-021-10020-1); [INESC TEC, unverified](https://repositorio.inesctec.pt/handle/123456789/7315)). These lacked GNSS referencing, and a 2023 scoping review notes that Nigeria's shortage of active GNSS stations leaves InSAR results quite uncertain ([Iku et al. 2023, unverified](https://www.dmsa.unipd.it/~teatini/Publications/Int_proceedings/Iku_etal23.html)). The weight of evidence favours rates of roughly 0–11 mm/yr with local hotspots. Even so, rates at the upper end are several times the current global-mean rate of sea-level rise. A verified study of Douala in Cameroon gives a useful regional benchmark. Land converted from tree cover to built-up since 2017 subsides at 7.1 mm/yr on average, against 1.9 mm/yr for land built up since 1992 ([Chounna Yemele et al. 2026](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/26/551/2026/)). If the same holds in Lagos, newly reclaimed land in Lekki, Banana Island and the lagoon fringe is subsiding fastest. No reclamation-specific Lagos rate has yet been published. AR6 grid projections do not capture this local subsidence, so they will understate relative sea-level rise on reclaimed ground.

### Rainfall extremes: the strong signal arrives after mid-century

The best verified source is Worou, Fichefet and Goosse (2023). Using 24 CMIP6 models under SSP5-8.5 for July–September over the Guinea coast, they project a **multimodel median increase of 21% in Rx5day (maximum five-day rainfall) and 48% in its year-to-year variability by 2080–2099**, relative to 1995–2014. Near-term (2021–2040) change in the mean is close to zero, and the mid-century (2041–2060) change is less robust ([Worou et al. 2023](https://wcd.copernicus.org/articles/4/511/2023/)). For a 2050 study this means CMIP6 gives only weak guidance on shifts in the mean. The more relevant hazard may be more frequent extreme years, as the variability result implies. Lagos floods are driven largely by short convective bursts, which coarse global models represent poorly, so Rx1day and sub-daily extremes need CORDEX-Africa or convection-permitting output. No Lagos-specific 2050 Rx1day or R95p projection was confirmed. The IPCC AR6 Interactive Atlas (West Africa region) and the Copernicus Climate Data Store's CMIP6 extreme indices are the places to get them.

### Land use and population: projections diverge on definitions

Population projections for 2050 range from about 16 million to about 37 million. The difference is mainly one of definition, not a real disagreement. World Bank and JRC calculations using the GHSL "urban centre" definition put Lagos at 12.8 million in 2025 and 16.2 million in 2050 ([Nairametrics reporting World Bank 2026](https://nairametrics.com/2026/10/30/world-bank-projects-luanda-could-overtake-lagos-as-sub-saharan-africas-most-populous-city-by-2050/)). Older figures based on UN agglomeration estimates, quoted in Nigerian media, give 17 million in 2025 and 36.9 million in 2050 (unverified; [Nairametrics 2025](https://nairametrics.com/2025/05/14/top-10-largest-cities-in-africa-by-population-2025/8/)). This study uses the GHSL urban-centre definition.

Land-use projections disagree more seriously. A CA-Markov study of Lagos State (Amaechi et al., unverified) projects built-up land growing by 664 km² between 2022 and 2050 while wetland shrinks by only 1.5 km² ([MDPI/RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gam:jsusta:v:16:y:2023:i:1:p:30-:d:1303287)). A Markov chain / Land Change Modeler study projects losses of **68.3% of wetlands** and 24.9% of forest between 2016 and 2031 (unverified; [MDPI Sci 2021](https://mdpi.com/2413-4155/3/2/23)). As noted, Aniramu et al. project wetland *growth* to 2050. Projections that differ by orders of magnitude, and even in direction, cannot be reconciled without reading the papers. The likely causes are class definitions and the Markov assumption that past transition rates continue unchanged, which breaks down once the most accessible wetland has already gone. Otuoze, Hunt and Jefferson (2021) used CA-Markov to project Lagos transport space to 2050, with kappa of 85–89%. They are an example of a Lagos model reported with some validation, though not with Figure of Merit ([Frontiers 2021](https://frontiersin.org/journals/future-transportation/articles/10.3389/ffutr.2021.673110/text)).

## A Landsat–Sentinel workflow in Earth Engine can close the record

### Free datasets and what each can and cannot do

Every dataset a study needs is free, and nearly all are available in Google Earth Engine (GEE). Landsat (from 1984) is the only consistent 30 m record covering the whole period. Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical imagery add 10 m detail from 2015. The radar matters in Lagos because rainy-season cloud makes optical imagery unreliable from about April to October. ### Random Forest in GEE as the primary classifier

The most defensible core method for one researcher is Random Forest (`ee.Classifier.smileRandomForest`, with 200–300 trees) applied to dry-season (November–March) median composites for epochs around 1986, 2000, 2013–2015, 2020 and 2025. These dates avoid most of the Landsat 7 data-gap problem. The feature stack should include the optical bands, NDVI, MNDWI, NDBI and a mangrove index, texture from grey-level co-occurrence (GLCM), FABDEM elevation, slope and height above nearest drainage (HAND), and, from 2015, Sentinel-1 VV, VH and their ratio. A six-class scheme fits the research question: open water, mangrove, freshwater swamp or herbaceous wetland, other vegetation, bare sand fill, and built-up. The bare sand-fill class is what makes reclamation visible, because NDBI alone confuses fresh sand with buildings. These recommendations are a synthesis of standard practice (Belgiu & Drăguţ 2016, unverified) rather than a single tested Lagos recipe. The closest regional precedent is a 10 m Sentinel-1/2 Random Forest wetland map of southern Nigeria, reported at about 88% accuracy but without Lagos-only figures ([Wetlands Ecology and Management 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/198420/13/Wetland%20mapping%20at%2010%20m%20resolution%20reveals%20fragmentation%20in%20southern%20Nigeria.pdf); accuracy figure unverified). **No Lagos-specific peer-reviewed study using Random Forest or Sentinel-2 to map wetland or mangrove change was found.**

Deep learning (U-Net and its variants) is worth trying only on the 10 m Sentinel epochs, where spatial context helps separate fresh fill from beach or stilt settlements from water. It needs about a thousand or more labelled image chips and a GPU outside GEE (for example free Colab). A 2025 Niger Delta preprint compared Res-UNet and DeepLabV3 with Random Forest and found Res-UNet with a ResNet152 backbone performed best of the deep models, but the head-to-head accuracies were not retrieved ([Onojeghuo et al. 2025](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202504.2146/v1)). For 1980s Landsat, with few labels and coarse pixels, the gain from deep learning is unlikely to justify the cost. A sensible design treats U-Net as an optional comparison on 2020 and 2025, scored on the same validation sample as Random Forest.

### Accuracy assessment that would survive peer review

Most existing Lagos studies report either no accuracy or only overall accuracy and kappa. The accepted standard is that of Olofsson et al. (2014): draw a stratified random sample from the final map using the map classes, and the rare change classes such as wetland-to-built, as strata, with roughly 50–100 points for each rare stratum; report the error matrix in area proportions with user's, producer's and overall accuracy; and report **area estimates with 95% confidence intervals** instead of pixel counts. Pontius and Millones (2011) recommend reporting quantity and allocation disagreement in place of kappa. Both references are canonical but were not re-fetched. Because the accuracy of a change map is roughly the product of the two date-map accuracies, sample-based area estimation matters most for exactly the numbers a reclamation study cares about. Reference labels for the 1980s must come from the Landsat imagery itself and from topographic maps, because high-resolution historical imagery for Lagos only starts around the 2000s. Training and validation samples should be separated spatially to avoid inflated scores.

### Dating fill events and modelling 2050

Post-classification comparison (from–to matrices between epochs) gives the reclamation account. LandTrendr or CCDC, both built into GEE, can date *when* each pixel was filled from annual Landsat composites. Wetland conversion in Lagos typically shows a characteristic sequence: vegetation clearance, then a bright, low-NDVI sand surface, then buildings. A segmentation algorithm run on NBR or MNDWI should pick up the year of filling. No published Lagos application of LandTrendr or CCDC was found, so this is itself new ground. JRC Global Surface Water transitions provide an independent check for open-lagoon fill up to 2021.

For 2050, CA-Markov (TerrSet or Python) and neural-network transition models (MOLUSCE in QGIS, Land Change Modeler) are the established choices in Nigerian studies. FLUS adds competition among several classes for scenario runs. Validation should take the form of a hindcast: calibrate on 2000→2013 or 2013→2020, predict 2025, and compare with the 2025 classified map. Report the Figure of Merit together with quantity and allocation disagreement, measured against a no-change null model (Pontius et al. 2008, unverified). Figure of Merit values of 10–30% are common and do not mean a model has failed. **No Figure of Merit has been published for any Lagos land-change model.** Three scenarios are natural for this topic: business as usual; wetland protection, which excludes remaining lagoon-fringe wetland and the Lekki Conservation Centre; and planned megaprojects, which forces conversion at Eko Atlantic and the Lekki Free Zone. Driver layers would include distance to roads and to the lagoon or coast, FABDEM elevation and HAND, GHSL density, WorldPop and protected areas.

### Estimating lost flood storage

The missing quantity in the literature is the volume of storage lost. It can be estimated as V = Σ max(0, WL_ref − z_pre) × A_pixel. The sum runs over every pixel that changed from wetland or water to fill or built-up. WL_ref is a reference water level, such as mean high water, a 1-in-10-year lagoon stage, or that stage plus AR6 2050 sea-level rise. z_pre is the ground elevation before filling. For land filled after about 2014, Copernicus DEM or FABDEM approximately records the pre-fill surface. For earlier fill, the DEM shows the raised surface, so z_pre has to be approximated, for example as the median elevation of remaining wetland within 500 m. FABDEM reduces mean absolute error relative to GLO-30 from 5.15 m to 2.88 m in forests and from 1.61 m to 1.12 m in built-up areas ([Hawker et al. 2022](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ac4d4f)). An independent comparison against 65 airborne lidar surveys ranked FABDEM first among free global DEMs ([Meadows et al. 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17538947.2024.2308734)). Even so, errors of one to two metres are as large as the storage depths involved in flat coastal Lagos. The result should therefore be presented as a Monte Carlo distribution (around 1,000 draws, perturbing z_pre by the DEM error) with a median and 5–95% range, alongside a simple lower bound of area multiplied by a typical wetland depth.

To show the scale involved, a simple illustration helps. This is arithmetic, not a published finding. Applying an assumed storage depth of 0.5–1 m to the roughly 248 km² of wetland lost on the eastern lagoon fringe between 1984 and 2006 gives about **120–250 million m³**. A properly constrained estimate of this kind, with uncertainty bounds, would be the first such figure for Lagos. Two cautions apply: global DEMs use the EGM2008 datum rather than the local Lagos datum, and they miss subsidence after they were acquired.

## Eight gaps this study can fill

The evidence above points to gaps that are specific, achievable with free data and a laptop, and relevant to current Lagos policy. The first is **a consistent time series**. Every published Lagos study uses two or three dates, with different boundaries and classes. A five-epoch or annual series from 1984 to 2025, with a fixed six-class scheme including bare fill, would be the first of its kind. The second is **rigorous accuracy and area estimation**. No published Lagos wetland study reports Olofsson-style area estimates with confidence intervals, and two of the key studies report no accuracy at all. The third is **resolving the disagreement over the direction of wetland change**. Reprocessing the 1984–2006 period with a common method would test whether the statewide wetland "gains" are artefacts, and would reconcile the Obiefuna and Aniramu figures. The fourth is **separating reclamation from other land conversion**. Isolating sand-fill polygons (Eko Atlantic, Banana Island, Lekki, Ikoyi foreshore) and dating them with LandTrendr would turn press and developer figures into independently measured ones. The fifth is **the first estimate of lost flood-storage volume**, with explicit DEM uncertainty.

The sixth gap is **evidence on the feedback loop**. Repeated classifications, combined with flood-extent maps from Sentinel-1 for recent events, could test whether plots that flood are more likely to be filled in the following years, and whether new fill is followed by flooding in adjacent cells. That would put numbers on a loop now known only from testimony. The seventh is **validated 2050 scenarios**. No Lagos land-change model has published a Figure of Merit, and existing wetland projections disagree by orders of magnitude. A hindcast-validated model run under protection and megaproject scenarios, overlain on AR6 sea-level values for Lagos and published subsidence rates, would give planners comparable futures. The eighth is **geographic coverage**. Western Lagos (Badagry, Ologe Lagoon, Ojo) and inland floodplains (Majidun, Isheri/Kara) are almost absent from the quantitative record.

The core of this study covers gaps one to five, with six and seven as extensions. The study area could be limited to the Lagos–Lekki lagoon system, to allow direct comparison with Obiefuna et al., or extended to the whole state if time allows. Dredging volumes, lagoon bathymetry and a joint rainfall–tide flood analysis would need field or agency data that are not freely available. They are treated as limitations of the study.

## Conclusion

The main lesson is that Lagos's wetland losses are certain in direction but uncertain in size. The flood consequences are argued from mechanism, not measured. The literature's most-cited numbers come from studies without accuracy assessment, and its most recent statewide study cannot agree with itself on whether wetland grew or shrank. That weakness is the opening for this study. A reproducible GEE pipeline with stratified area estimates would correct the record. Converting area into storage volume would give the first figure planners can weigh directly against drainage investment.

The 2050 evidence also shifts where attention should go. Before mid-century, the choice of emissions scenario changes sea level less than subsidence on soft, recently filled ground does. CMIP6 rainfall signals for the Guinea coast are weak until late in the century, apart from rising year-to-year variability. The most consequential variable for Lagos flood risk to 2050 is therefore what happens to the remaining lagoon-fringe land: whether it is filled, and how fast the fill settles. That makes reclamation a planning choice within the city's control, unlike the climate variables, and puts a land-change study of this kind at the centre of the problem.
